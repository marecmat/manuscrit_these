\chapter{Dispersion relations of leaky guided waves in metaporous layers}
\label{sec:prb}
    {\color{gray}{This chapter is constituted from an article in preparation for publication in Physical Review B and is authored by \textit{Mathieu Maréchal, Olivier Dazel, Vicent Romero-Garc\'ia and Jean-Philippe Groby}. The contents of the paper will not submitted as is and will undergo minor changes, without affecting the core scientific content of this chapter.}}

\begin{abstract}
    A porous layer with embedded periodic rigid inclusions coupled to semi-infinite media is investigated, which particular interest in this work is on the coupling between the periodic unit cell and the surrounding fluid and the computation of the dispersion relation of such system. The porous unit cell is modeled as an equivalent fluid with inherent viscothermal dissipations. The quasi-guided waves propagating in the periodic material are able to radiate from the layer to surrounding media, leading to energy leakage. A numerical method is presented to calculate the leaky complex waves in the system, based on a finite element discretisation of the unit cell coupled using a state-vector formalism to a plane wave modelling of the surrounding fluid. Finally, an iteratively solved linear system is used to compute the dispersion relation. Studying first an homogeneous porous layer allows for the classification in terms of proper and improper solution of the solution spectrum in such dissipative layers. Results include cases for a cylindrical inclusion tuned to allow for perfect absorption at a given frequency as well as split-ring resonators.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
Achieving the control of wave propagation is the main objective driving the development of phononic crystals and acoustic metamaterials. (These materials can achieve various effects, including significant sound attenuation, broadband absorption, and controlled diffusion due to their geometrical and material properties). Over the past decade, metaporous structures have been sought for their enhanced acoustic absorption properties. Notably it was shown that total absorption occurred at a subwavelength frequency by tuning the inclusion radius \cite{groby2011, weisser2016}, from an effect emerging from the embedded periodic inclusion. Resonant inclusions have shown to be able to produce an additional absorption peak as well \cite{cavalieri2021}. 

These configurations combine spatial periodicity by addition of a scatterer in the unit cell with the intrinsic material dissipations of porous and poroelastic materials, resulting in complex wave dispersion phenomena. While significant progress has been made in calculating the band structures for closed 1D periodic systems \cite{laude2009, magliacano2020}, existing approaches often neglect the coupling between the structure and the surrounding medium that exist in practical cases. Such coupling introduces energy leakage from the structure to the surrounding medium; modes in such configurations thus become leaky \cite{marcuvitz1956, goldstone1959}. These leaky guided waves are modes that partially radiate energy into a surrounding medium, and are not addressed in the context of lossy periodic materials. This gap becomes critical when accurate modelling of dispersion is required to capture both material losses and radiation leakage, as encountered in real-world acoustic devices but also in the case of a computation of the acoustic response of the structure, where a plane wave originated from an air medium impinges on said structure.

Diverse methods are able to compute the acoustic response of such structure like the multiple scattering theory (MST), used for porous \cite{groby2011} and metaporoelastic \cite{weisser2016} structures validated using classical finite elements method (FEM). The FEM with high-order finite elements \cite{beriot2016,lieu2016} has been addressed for various acoustic problems. Coupled to a transfer matrix formalism, it allows for the study of homogeneous multilayer configurations \cite{chevillotte2011, alimonti2015} and periodic structures when coupled to Bloch waves \cite{gaborit2018}.

Dispersion in such structures originates from the interaction of waves with geometrical obstacles and the inherent losses in these materials. The computation of the dispersion relations in periodic structure has long relied on Plane Wave Expansion (PWE) and its extended version (EPWE) \cite{romero-garcia2010, laude2009}. However, their use case is limited to materials of the same nature and to lossless materials respectively. FEM has been tackled as well to solve the dispersion relations of a 3D structure of porous material with rigid inclusions \cite{magliacano2020}. To our knowledge, using multiple scattering for the dispersion problem might be unfeasible since the Schlömilch series arising in the problem \cite{groby2011, weisser2016} diverges when the longitudinal wavenumber becomes complex.

The dispersion of waves in periodic structure containing a porous or poroelastic host has been studied e.g. in a metaporous liner, highlighting the existence of an exceptional point in the presence of a resonant inclusion \cite{xiong2017}. A formulation is presented in Ref.~\cite{wang2019} for the computation of a dispersion relation in a 1D periodic poroelastic alternating medium. The complex dispersion curve of a 2D phononic crystal with an anisotropic poroelastic host has been studied \cite{wang2020}, as well as with different interface effects \cite{zhang2023}.

Accounting for leaky modes leads to a more accurate modelling of the structure in the presence of a surrounding medium. Various numerical methods among which the spectral collocation method (SCM) or semi-analytical finite elements (SAFE) have been introduced to compute the complex dispersion curves in homogeneous layers with leakage in elastic layers \cite{kiefer2019, georgiades2022}, viscoelastic \cite{kiefer2020} and in multilayer dissipative structures \cite{marechal2025}. The discretisation in 1D done in this framework cannot be generalized to periodic structures wherein a 2D discretisation is required.

Structures composed of an array of scatterers have also been investigated and their support for Rayleigh-Bloch waves has been highlighted in several works in the last decades \cite{porter1999,bennetts2022,matsushima2024,laude2025}.

Leaky guided waves in lossy periodic layers has not been addressed in acoustics. Leaky waves in lossy structures was discussed in optics for plasmonic nanospheres \cite{campione2011}. However, related works in acoustics include Ref.~\cite{laude2013} where material losses in phononic crystals are studied with dispersion diagrams, and Ref.~\cite{magliacano2020} where the dispersion of waves in a 3D periodic porous layer is shown, but no leakage is accounted for in either of these papers. Computation of such dispersion relation using Comsol would result in a tedious formulation involving some perfectly-matched layer (PML) as done in homogeneous structures \cite{gallezot2017, mazzotti2014, mazzotti2017} and thus the need of additionally discretizing the surrounding medium and would also lead to additional radiation modes and cavity resonances. 

We propose a method that involves a finite element discretisation of the unit cell coupled to a plane-wave description of its surrounding half-spaces. The coupling at the interfaces is imposed using a state vector formalism. The method will be first presented in Section.~\ref{sec:method} with the analytical description of the air surrounding media and the procedure for its coupling with the periodic structure modeled using FEM. This problem is then solved using a root-finding method to retrieve the dispersion relation of said structure. Then, the case of an homogeneous porous layer will be discussed in Section.~\ref{sec:homog_layer} in order to highlight on one hand the key modal behaviour of this simple geometry and on the other hand the classification proposed for these complex modes, which is not a trivial question. Finally, Section.~\ref{sec:results} will show some results with a rigid inclusion, a configuration showing perfect acoustic absorption and a split-ring resonator. A comparison between the modal behaviour and the acoustic response of the structure is done, highlighting the specific modes involved in the scattering processes.


\section{Method}\label{sec:method} 

\subsection{Problem presentation}
\label{ssec:pb_pres}
In all this paper, geometrical coordinates are denoted by $\mbf x = (x_1, x_2)$ and a positive time dependence $e^{\im \omega t}$ is considered. The unit cell, as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:schema} is a 2D rectangle of thickness $h$ and width $d$ (corresponding to the period of the unit cell). Hence, the considered layer is constituted by  infinitely repeated cells in the $x_1$-direction. The host material $\Omega$ is a rigid-frame porous layer modeled as an equivalent fluid, which response is governed by Helmholtz equation with complex fluid properties, density $\rho_{eq}$ and compressibility $K_{eq}$, both obtained from the JCA model \cite{johnson1987, champoux1991} as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:jca}. A rigid inclusion of arbitrary shape is embedded in the cell. The top and bottom edges are respectively denoted by $\Gamma_t$ and $\Gamma_b$ and they can be associated to a Neumann boundary condition (rigid wall) or to the interface with a semi-infinite air domain (in the $x_2$ direction). The method will focus on the transmission case, that is both top and bottom interfaces coupled to the semi-infinite air domain.

The proposed method is based on modelling the semi-infinite media using a plane wave formalism (presented in \cref{wave-semi_infinte}) and a model of the periodic cell using FEM (presented in \cref{fem-periodic}). The final homogeneous system that describes the coupled problem for the transmission case is derived in \cref{coupling-models} as well as the modification allowing to write the system for the rigidly-backed case, that is an addition of a rigid boundary condition at $\Gamma_t$.

\subsection{\label{wave-semi_infinte}Wave formalism in the semi-infinite media}

The pressure fields, solutions to the Helmholtz equation in the semi-infinite air domains, can be expressed as a countable superposition of Bloch modes. For each of them (indexed by $q\in\mathbb Z$), a decoupling of the space variables $x_1$ and $x_2$ is considered. The dependence in $x_1$ is thereby associated to a function,
\begin{equation}
\psi_q(x_1) = e^{-\im k_{1,q} x_1},\quad k_{1, q} = k_1 + 2 \pi q / d. 
\end{equation}
The longitudinal wavenumber remains to be determined and can be written as $k_1 = k_1'+\im k_1''$. Its real part $k_1'$ is related to the propagation of the phase flow while the imaginary part $k_1''$ represents the attenuation of the wave when negative. The amplitudes of each pressure field in the top and bottom semi-infinite media are respectively denoted as $a^t_q$ and $a^b_q$. The expressions for the top and bottom pressures can be written in the form of outgoing waves,
\begin{equation}
        p^t(\mbf x) = \sum_{q \in \mathbb Z} a^{t}_q \psi_q(x_1) e^{ - \im k_{2, q} (x_2 - h)}, \quad p^b(\mbf x) = \sum_{q \in \mathbb Z} a^{b}_q \psi_q(x_1) e^{+ \im k_{2, q} x_2},
    \label{eq:out_waves}
\end{equation}
where the mode index $q$ is associated to a transverse wavenumber $k_{2, q} =  \sqrt{k^2_{0} - k^2_{1, q}} $, with $k_{0} = \omega / c_0$ the wavenumber in air and $c_0$ its wave velocity. This wavenumber can also be written in algebraic form as $k_{2, q}= k_{2, q}' + \im k_{2, q}''$. The assumptions on the signs that $k_{2,q}'$ and $k_{2,q}''$ should take depend on the choice of the square root branch cut and are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:homog_layer}. At this point, we only select forward wave solutions propagating towards positive $x_1$ direction, that is $k_1' > 0$ since the spectrum of solutions for backward waves is symmetric in this reciprocal system. \\

In order to couple the periodic layer and the semi-infinite layers, a state vector formalism is introduced. This vector gathers  the normal displacement along $x_2$, $u_2$ and the pressure field linked to the latter by the Euler equation, $\rho_0\omega^2 u_2 = \partial_2 p$, with $\rho_0$, the density of air. At the top interface, the state vector can be decomposed on Bloch waves and reads as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S^top}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mbf S^t(x_1) &=
        \begin{pmatrix} u_2(x_1,x_2=h) \\ p(x_1,x_2=h) \end{pmatrix}
        = \sum_{q \in \mathbb Z} \mbf S_q^t \psi_q(x_1), \\
        \mbf S_q^t &= \begin{pmatrix}
            S_{1,q}^t \\ S_{2,q}^t
        \end{pmatrix} = \mbf \sigma_q^t a_q^t = \begin{pmatrix} -\cfrac{ik_{2,q}}{\rho_0 \omega^2} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} a_q^t,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mbf S_q^t$ is the state vector associated to the $q$th Bloch wave and $\mbf \sigma_q^t $ its polarisation vector. A similar expression can be obtained for the bottom interface,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:S^bottom}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mbf S^b(x_1) &=
        \begin{pmatrix} u_2(x_1,x_2=0) \\ p(x_1,x_2=0) \end{pmatrix}
        = \sum_{q \in \mathbb Z} \mbf S_q^b \psi_q(x_1), \\
        \mbf S_q^b &= \begin{pmatrix}
            S_{1,q}^b \\ S_{2,q}^b
        \end{pmatrix} = \mbf \sigma_q^b a_q^b = \begin{pmatrix} \cfrac{ik_{2,q}}{\rho_0 \omega^2} \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} a_q^b.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In the following, we will derive a state vector expression at both interfaces in the unit cell to couple both domains. This will be done using the fields involved in the FEM formulation.

\subsection{\label{fem-periodic}Finite-element approach}

The weak formulation of the Helmholtz equation \cite{dhatt2005} reads as
\begin{equation}
        \underbrace{\frac 1 {\rho_0 \omega^2} \int_\Omega \nabla p \nabla \delta p \, d\Omega  - \frac 1 {\rho_0 c_0^2} \int_\Omega p \delta p \, d\Omega}_{\mathcal I_{\Omega} (p,\delta p)} \quad - \int_{\partial \Omega} u_n(M) \,\delta p(M)\,dS = 0, \quad \forall\, \delta p \in V,
        \label{eq:weak_form_helmholtz}
\end{equation}
with $V$ is a set of admissible test functions. $\mathcal I_{\Omega} (p,\delta p)$ corresponds to the volume term. The pressure field $p$ is associated to the unknown function while the normal outward displacement field $u_n$ is associated to boundaries. The volume terms will be discretized using a classical FEM procedure \cite{atalla2015,solin2004} and won't then be detailed in this paper. A focus is put on the boundary term, which is separated into five different regions denoted as $\Gamma_l$, $\Gamma_r$, $\Gamma_t$, $\Gamma_b$, and $\Gamma_i$ respectively for the left, right, top, bottom and interior boundaries. The inclusion being rigid, the normal velocity at boundary $\Gamma_i$ is zero and thus the associated boundary term is zero. We then finally focus on the external boundary of the periodic cell, and the top interface is first considered. The associated boundary term $\mathcal I_t$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
        \mathcal I_t = \int_{\Gamma_t} u_2(x_1)\delta p(x_1) dx_1 = \int_{\Gamma_t}\left( \sum_{q \in \mathbb Z} \,S^{t}_{1,q} \psi_q(x_1)  \right)\delta p(x_1) dx_1
    \label{eq:top_boundary},
\end{equation}
where $S^t_{1, q}$ is the first component of $\mbf S^t_q$ defined in \cref{eq:S^top}. A boundary term for $\Gamma_b$ denoted $\mathcal I_b$ is defined similarly as $\mathcal I_t$. Therefore, the weak form in Eq.~(\ref{eq:weak_form_helmholtz}) can be rewritten as,
\begin{equation}
        \mathcal I_{\Omega_1} (p,\delta p) + \mathcal I_t + \mathcal I_b = - \int_{\Gamma_{r}} u_2^{r}(x_2) \delta p (x_2)\,dx_2 - \int_{\Gamma_{l}} u_2^{l}(x_2) \delta p (x_2) \,dx_2.
        \label{eq:weak_form_bloch}
\end{equation}

Since we introduced a state vector notation for the fields at the interface, we need also need to express an expansion on Bloch modes for the pressure field in order to provide the required new set of equations. Making use of the orthogonality properties of Bloch modes allows to write a relation such that, $\forall \,q' \in \mathbb Z$,
\begin{equation}
        \quad\int_{\Gamma_t} \left(\sum_{q \in \mathbb Z} p(x_1) \psi_{q'}^{-1}(x_1) \right) \,dx_1 = S^t_{2,q'} d,
    \label{eq:bloch_ortho_top}
\end{equation}
with $d$ the periodicity and $S_{2,q'}^t$ the second component of $\mbf S_{q'}^t$. In this expression, the orthogonality of Bloch modes is satisfied using $\psi_{q'}^{-1}(x_1)= e^{ik_{1,q'}x_1}$ instead of the usual complex conjugate, due to the complex nature of the Bloch wavenumber.

In summary, two main expressions were derived. The first one in Eq.~(\ref{eq:weak_form_bloch}) contains the weak form and expresses the first component of the state vector (displacement involved in the surface term) $S_{1,q}^t$ at the top and $ S_{1,q}^b$ at the bottom interface, while Eq.~(\ref{eq:bloch_ortho_top}) expresses the second component of the state vector (pressure involved in the volume term as nodal values) $S_{2,q}^t$ at the top interface $S_{2,q}^b$ at the bottom interface. \\

The discretisation process involves multiple steps regarding both the Bloch wave basis and the finite element formulation. First, the infinite sums over $q\in \mathbb Z$ involved in the previous expressions are truncated so that $q = -Q,\ldots0\ldots Q$, with $Q$ accounting for the necessary integer number of Bloch to take into the sum. This discrete basis is denoted $\mbf \Psi(x_1) = \sum_{q =-Q}^Q \psi_q(x_1)$, and contains $\mathcal Q = 2Q+1$ values. additionally, using the standard procedure in finite elements, the nodal values and test functions are discretized using second-order shape functions contained in matrix $\mbf N(\mbf x)$. The pressure field is thus $p(x_1) = \mbf N(x_1) \mbf p$ the test function is ${\delta p} = \mbf N(x_1) \mbf {\delta p}$.

Finally, putting together the discretized equations allows to write a full system of equations for the discretized problem. It has three main rows; the first and last set of equations respectively contains the discrete Bloch orthogonality relation discretized from Eq.~(\ref{eq:bloch_ortho_top}), while the second row contains the discretized weak form in Eq.~(\ref{eq:weak_form_bloch}). The system is composed of block matrices which indices relate to either the top or bottom interface and index $i$ relates to the inner nodal values. Denoting $\mbf S^t = ( S_{-Q}^t, \ldots, S_Q^t )^\transp$ and $\mbf S^b = ( S_{-Q}^b, \ldots, S_Q^b )^\transp$, the system thus writes as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \mbf D_{tt} & \mbf D_{ti} & \mbf 0      \\
        \mbf D_{it} & \mbf D_{ii} & \mbf D_{ib} \\
        \mbf 0      & \mbf D_{bi} & \mbf D_{bb} \\
    \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
       \mbf S^t \\ \mbf p \\ \mbf S^b
    \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
        0 \\ \mbf f \\ 0
    \end{pmatrix}. 
    \label{eq:discrete_system}
\end{equation}
The first row contains matrices that can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mbf D_{ti} = \int_{\Gamma_t} \mbf \Psi^{-1} (x_1) \mbf N(x_1) \,dx_1, \quad \mbf D_{tt} = - d \mbf I.
\end{equation}
Similar forms can be obtained for $\mbf D_{bi}$ and $\mbf D_{bb}$. In the second set of equations, $\mbf D_{ii}$ is the discretized volume term with size $(n_{dof}, n_{dof})$ that only depends on the degrees of freedom (DOF) vector $\mbf p$ and,
\begin{equation}
     \mbf D_{it} = \int_{\Gamma_t}\mbf N^\transp(x_1) \mbf \Psi(x_1) \,dx_1, 
\end{equation}
is the discretized surface term for the top interface which is $(n_{dof}, 2\cdot \mathcal Q)$. A similar form can be found for $\mbf D_{ib}$. In Eq.~\ref{eq:discrete_system}, $\mbf p$ contains the inner nodal pressure values and the right-hand side $\mbf f$ contains the nodal force vector acting on the nodal values, the discrete form of the right-hand side of Eq.~(\ref{eq:weak_form_bloch}) related to the right and left boundaries surface terms. Each of the state vector $\mbf S^t$, $\mbf S^b$ has size $(2\mathcal Q, 1)$.

In the cell, the displacement field from the right-hand side boundary $u_r$ is related to that of the left side $u_l$, periodically modulated by $\Delta = e^{-\im k_{1,q} d}$. The physical periodicity is thus written as $\mbf u_r = \Delta \mbf u_l$. Similarly, the periodicity relates the pressures fields between the left and right boundaries as $\mbf p^r = \Delta \mbf p^l$. Among other methods for the implementation of periodic boundary conditions in FEM \cite{langlet1995, mace2005}, we propose our own implementation. In the previously derived system, the nodal values vector can be written as $\mbf p= \left(\mbf p^l \,\,  \mbf p^i \,\,  \mbf p^r \right)^\transp$ which contains respectively the pressure values at the left boundary, the inner DOFs and at the right boundary, respectively. Applying the periodicity relations, it follows that 
\begin{equation}
    \mbf{\hat P}\mbf D \mbf P\begin{pmatrix}
        \mbf p^l \\ \mbf p^i
    \end{pmatrix} = \mbf{\hat P}\begin{pmatrix}
        \mbf u^l \\ \mbf 0 \\  \Delta \mbf u^l
    \end{pmatrix},
    \label{eq:periodicity}
\end{equation}
where rows of $\mbf D$ are multiplied by $\mbf{\hat P}$ and its columns are multiplied by $\mbf P$, both of which are expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mbf P = \begin{pmatrix} \mbf I & 0 \\ 0 & \mbf I \\ \Delta \mbf I & 0 \end{pmatrix},\quad 
    \mbf{\hat P} = 
        \begin{pmatrix}
            \mbf I & 0 & -\Delta^{-1} \mbf I \\ 0 & \mbf I & 0 
        \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Doing so allows the right-hand side from Eq.~(\ref{eq:periodicity}) to vanish, and yields an homogeneous linear system.


\subsection{\label{coupling-models} Final homogeneous linear system}

The discrete system can be further manipulated so that most of the inner DOF related to $p^i$ from the FE formulations are condensed. In a way, this comes back to writing a transfer matrix relating the top state vector to the bottom state vector. Further manipulation of the condensed matrix will therefore be much more computationally effective, due to the drastic reduction in matrix size. The matrix $\mbf{\tilde D} = \mbf{\hat P}\mbf D \mbf P$ denotes the full system and the $\,\tilde{}\,$ symbol denotes that the periodicity has been imposed on the matrix or vector. Let us write that the nodal values $\tilde{\mbf p} = (\mbf p_l, \, \mbf p_i)^\transp$ can be written as a combination of $\mbf S^t$ and $\mbf S^b$ by manipulating Eq.~(\ref{eq:discrete_system}) as,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mbf p} = \mbf R_t \mbf S^t + \mbf R_b \mbf S^b, 
    \label{eq:condensation_dof}
\end{equation}
with $\mbf R_t = - \tilde{\mbf D}_{ii}^{-1} \tilde{\mbf D}_{it}$, $\mbf R_b = - \tilde{\mbf D}_{ii}^{-1} \tilde{\mbf D}_{ib}$ and the indices used for those matrices are the same as the blocks of Eq.~(\ref{eq:discrete_system}). The following system can thus be derived,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}
            \tilde{\mbf D}_{tt} + \tilde{\mbf D}_{ti} \mbf R_t & \tilde{\mbf D}_{ti}\mbf R_b \\
            \tilde{\mbf D}_{bi}\mbf R_t & \tilde{\mbf D}_{bb} + \tilde{\mbf D}_{bi} \mbf R_b
        \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
            \mbf S^t \\ \mbf S^b 
        \end{pmatrix} = 
        \mbf M^t \mbf S^t + \mbf M^b \mbf S^b = 0, 
        \label{eq:condenS^tb}
\end{equation}
which is an expression that relates the fields from the upper interface to those at the lower interface. Using the latter as well as the expressions relating the semi-infinite domain wave amplitudes with the state vector in the second line of Eq.~(\ref{eq:S^top}) and Eq.~(\ref{eq:S^bottom}), the final linear system is obtained as,
\begin{equation}
    \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}
        \mbf \sigma^t & -\mbf I & \mbf 0 & \mbf 0 \\
        \mbf 0 & \mbf M^t & \mbf M^b & \mbf 0 \\
        \mbf 0 & \mbf 0 & -\mbf I & \mbf \sigma^b \\
    \end{pmatrix}    }_{\mbf M}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \mbf a^t \\ \mbf S^t \\ \mbf S^b \\ \mbf a^b \\ 
    \end{pmatrix} = \mbf 0.
    \label{eq:final_system}
\end{equation}
In this case, the final system matrix size does not depend upon the FE discretisation number of DOFs. The matrices $\mbf M^b$, $\mbf M^t$ each have a size $(4\mathcal Q, 2\mathcal Q)$, both $\mbf \sigma^t$ and $\mbf \sigma^b$ have size $(\mathcal Q, \,\mathcal Q)$, the identity matrix is $(\mathcal Q, \,2\mathcal Q)$ and the state vectors are $(2\mathcal Q, \,1)$. Thus, the final system has a size $(6\mathcal Q, \,6\mathcal Q)$.

In order to apply rigid boundary conditions on either one of the interface to obtain a rigidly-backed configuration, the same method can be applied by taking $\mathcal I_t = 0$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:weak_form_bloch}) and thus the expression for $\mbf S^t$ is removed. 

The final matrix system that arise is iteratively solved using a root-finding method such that, 
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{det}(\mbf M(k_1, \omega)) = 0. 
    \label{eq:M_det}
\end{equation}
While an eigenvalue problem formulation would be much more efficient and reliable, this seems unfeasible due to the content of the block matrices of the system, especially the dependence of the terms on both $e^{\pm k_{1q}d}$ and $e^{\pm k_{1q}x_1}$, leading to a possibly complicated procedure to write an eigenvalue problem on $k_{1q}$. The root-finding procedure is based on the Müller method, which iteratively converges to a zero of the determinant of the matrix, leading to the modes of the systems. Extension of it allows to retrieve the mode shapes, as done in Ref.~\cite{marechal2025}.

An intermediate validation case is presented in Appendix~\ref{app:neumann_bc}. Neumann boundary conditions are applied at both top and bottom interfaces. The dispersion relation is presented for this simple case and is compared to both a generalized eigenvalue problem formulated from the same method (only valid in this particular case) and a Comsol model comparison. Presented results appear similar to those presented in Ref.~\cite{magliacano2020}

The computation of the acoustic response of the considered structures can also be achieved by modifying slightly the method; the matrix system described Eq.~(\ref{eq:final_system}) can be modified by adding a right-hand side state vector corresponding to the incident wave at the rows associated to the $\mbf a^b$ wave amplitudes, which can thus be understood as the reflection coefficients, while $\mbf a^t$ becomes the transmission coeffcients. Details for this variation of the method are given in Appendix~\ref{app:direct_pb}

\section{Classification of solutions}
\label{sec:homog_layer}
\subsection{Proper and improper modes}

All solutions obtained from Eq.~(\ref{eq:M_det}) are mathematical solutions; only a subset of those are physical modes that actually represent the modal behaviour of the studied configuration. In order to sort out non-physical solutions, a classification is therefore proposed in this section depending on their locii in the complex plane. Considering the case of an homogeneous layer, there are no Bloch modes in this case. Thus, we study the signs of real and imaginary component of $k_1=k_1' + i k_1''$ and their corresponding transverse wavenumber in the fluid, $k_{2} = \pm \sqrt{k_0^2 - k_1^2} = k_2' + \im k_2''$. As explained in Section \ref{ssec:pb_pres}, backward waves are not sought since the wavenumber spectrum is symmetric with respect to the $k_1' = 0$ plane. 

In the case of leaky waves originated from a lossless layer, the selection of the branch cuts of the wavenumber is straightforward and has been widely discussed in the literature \cite{goldstone1959,monticone2015}; they take the form of outgoing growing waves in the semi-infinite medium. However, the challenges faced with this configuration resides in the coupling in the system between two losses mechanisms that are viscothermal losses in the layer and radiation losses due to the fluid coupling.

The classification defined in Ref.~\cite{campione2011}, in which complex wavenumber solutions to a problem involving both material losses and radiation to a surrounding medium are categorized into either \textit{proper} or \textit{improper} modes, is adopted for the present work. Starting from the Snell-Descartes relation between the wavenumbers, stating that $k_1^2 + k_{2,0}^2 = k_0^2$ and taking the imaginary part of that equation yields,
\begin{equation}
    k_1' k_1'' = -k_{2,0}' k_{2,0}'',    
    \label{eq:im_snell}
\end{equation}
which should hold for all cases \cite{treyssede2014}. Restricting {proper} solutions $k_1'> 0$ and $k_1'' < 0$ (wave attenuated along the layer in the $x_1$ direction), leads to either $k_{2,0}' k_{2,0}'' < 0$ or $k_{2,0}'k_{2,0}'' > 0$. The latter is regarded as an {improper} solution due to the growing nature of the mode in the semi-infinite domain (positive imaginary part).

These categories of the encountered wave solutions are presented in diagrams in Fig.~\ref{fig:classification}a and \ref{fig:classification}b, respectively for $k_1$ and $k_2$ complex plane. Each quadrant represents the solution whether it is forward or backward, growing or attenuated, outgoing or ingoing. Three types of solutions are also highlighted in Fig.~\ref{fig:classification}c-e, of which only one is considered as a {proper} solution. In Fig.~\ref{fig:classification}d, mode I corresponds to an attenuated field in the layer with ingoing attenuated radiation. The two other types of solutions are deemed {improper}: II is a growing field in the layer with ingoing growing radiation and III is an attenuated field in the layer with outgoing growing radiation respectively in Fig.~\ref{fig:classification}e and Fig.~\ref{fig:classification}f. 

\subsection{Case of a lossless homogeneous layer coupled to a half-space on one side}

To highlight the modal behaviour of the system and the coupling between the various loss mechanisms, we start by studying a rigidly-backed homogeneous porous layer of thickness $h$ with a fluid half-space above. This configuration is described using its dispersion equation, 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{k_{2,0}}{\rho_0} \cos(k_{2,eq}h) + \im \frac{k_{2,eq}}{\rho_{eq}} \sin(k_{2,eq}h) = 0, 
    \label{eq:homog_disp_eq}
\end{equation}
onto which solutions for $k_{20}$ are sought, with $k_1 = + \sqrt{k_{0}^2 - k_1^2}$, and $k_{2,eq} = + \sqrt{k_{eq}^2 - k_1^2}$. Choosing this convention for $k_1$ leads to selecting forward propagating waves $k_1' > 0$, an arbitrary choice that yields a set of solutions reciprocal to $k_1' < 0$ which corresponds to backward (left) propagating waves. The root-finding procedure is applied to Eq.~(\ref{eq:homog_disp_eq}) and retrieve all possible solutions for complex values of $k_{20}$ in a given frequency range. The parameters for the JCA model giving the effective densities $\rho_{eq}$ and compressibilities $K_{eq}$ for the porous layer are given in Table~\ref{tab:poroelastic_params} and its effective parameters are given in Appendix \ref{app:jca}. The saturating fluid and that occupying the half-space is air with density $\rho_f = 1.213\textrm{ kg.m}^{-3}$, the heat capacity ratio $\kappa = 1.4$, kinematic compressibility $\mu_f = 1.839 \times 10^{-5} \textrm{ Pa.s}$, the Prandtl number $\textrm{Pr} = 0.71$, and adiabatic bulk modulus $\kappa P_0$, where the atmospheric pressure is $P_0 = 1.013 \times 10^5 \textrm{ Pa}$.

The layer has a thickness $h = 50~\mathrm{mm}$. At first, the dispersion relation is computed for a lossless porous layer, that is keeping only the real components of $\rho_{eq}$ and $K_{eq}$ and thus $k_{eq} = k'_{eq} \in \mathbb R$. The obtained solutions are presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:lossless_porous} for $k_1$ and $k_{20}$ wavenumber in selected views of the complex plane. Each set of solution throughout the remaining of this paper is shown as lines which have been tracked from the raw data using a method akin to the MAC sorting criterion \cite{vacher2010, pastor2012} with a few manual fixes for outlier points and coalescent solutions. Note that since a root-finding procedure is used, results are not as robust as solving a set of eigenvalues. The iterative scheme leads to a few seldom missing solutions, occurring e.g. for iterations trapped in local minima or when the algorithm is unable to converge for reasons due to a particular shape of the function in given sparse locations. These unavoidable missed solutions are seemingly mostly dependant on the matrix conditioning.

In Fig.~\ref{fig:lossless_porous}a and Fig.~\ref{fig:lossless_porous}d, the non-zero imaginary part of the dispersion relation in $k_1$ or $k_{20}$ are solely due to radiation losses as the dissipation in the porous layer is not accounted for. Nevertheless, the homogeneous layer remains slightly dispersive as the real components of the fluid parameters computed with the JCA model faintly varies with frequency, thus explaining the dissymetry in Fig.~\ref{fig:lossless_porous}e. In Fig.~\ref{fig:lossless_porous}b, mode I is always propagating ($k_1' \in (k_0, k_{eq})$) and always proper as it represents a purely guided wave radiating as a purely evanescent wave in the air domain. Modes II and III have a large $k_1''$ and are both improper below the cutoff frequency (around $k_0 h \approx 5$) as seen on Fig.~\ref{fig:lossless_porous}e as they respectively correspond to a growing wave in the layer with exponentially growing radiation and an attenuated wave in the layer with exponentially growing radiation. Above the cutoff, mode III is still improper for the same reasons and mode II changes $k_{20}''$ sign, becoming proper with the same behaviour to that of mode I (purely guided wave). 

For modes II and III, $k_1''$ is large at lower frequencies, meaning that radiation losses are prominent in this frequency range and that no energy is radiated from the layer past the cut-on frequency since $k_1'' = 0$. Additionally, the I mode is unaffected by these losses regardless of the frequency. As an amount of 1\% of losses are introduced in the layer (that is $k_{eq} = k'_{eq} + 0.01\cdot \im  k''_{eq}$) in Fig.~\ref{fig:lossless_porous}c, the point where the 2 modes coincide is lost, and the 2 branches are fully separated, both wavenumbers $k_1$ and $k_{20}$ become fully complex over the whole frequency range. The symmetry between the positive and negative $k_1''$ planes is thus lost because of the introduction of losses into the system. These results are reminiscent of those obtained by Laude \etal \cite{laude2013} who showed the splitting of the left and right-propagating Bloch wave in a crystal lattice dispersion relation by the addition of losses.

\subsection{Lossy homogeneous porous layer}
The next step is the addition of the full losses to the porous layer, with results presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:losses_porous}. In this case, the symmetries shown for modes II and III the lossless case in Fig.~\ref{fig:lossless_porous}a and Fig.~\ref{fig:lossless_porous}d with respect to the $k'_{20}$ and $k''_1$ planes are lost. The introduction of the viscothermal losses in the porous layer leads all modes to be somewhat attenuated, as expected. It can first be observed that $k_{20}$ is shifted for both the real and imaginary part for all branches. However, the proper part of mode II shifts towards negative values, indicating an attenuated field in the fluid half-space $k_{2,0}'' < 0$ but also an ingoing wave $k_{2,0}' < 0$, which is a consequence of Eq.~\ref{eq:im_snell}.

Mode I keeps the same trend to that of the lossless case with a small $k_1'' < 0$ value in Fig.~\ref{fig:losses_porous}c) and is shifted to negative $k_{20}'$. Mode II follows the same shift as mode I and remains \textit{improper} below its cutoff frequency, identified in this case by a change of sign in $k_1''$ and $k_{20}''$.
Finally, Fig.~\ref{fig:losses_porous}g presents the error on the calculated dispersion solutions for modes I and II between the analytical results discussed previously and those obtained by the FE formulation of the presented method. While both depending on the developed root-finding algorithm, the solution show low error which is increasing with frequency, expected to depend on the mesh size. The error for both shown branches appear to be the same after mode II (in pink) is above the cut-off frequency.

In the study of leaky modes in lossless layers, as has been done for elastic materials in several papers, both modes II and III could be selected equivalently, and corresponds to either a growing solution in the layer which implies an ingoing growing wave in the fluid (backward leaky waves), or an attenuated solution in the layer with outgoing growing wave in the fluid (forward leaky waves). In this configuration, the latter is chosen for its more physically meaningful behaviour. However, the desymmetrisation of these two modes in the case of a porous layer with losses means that the selection of the branch cut is not straightforward. The choice of the branch cut is made so that mode II is selected as a proper solution, with $k_1'' < 0$ and $k_{20}'' < 0$, which is considered the physically meaningful solution in our case. Mode III will be thus discarded as a solution in upcoming results as it is always improper.

\subsection{Dispersion relation of a unit cell with rigid inclusions}
\label{ssec:rigid_incl}
Results related to periodic geometry, i.e., metaporous surfaces/interfaces, are presented in this section. A rigid cylindrical inclusion with radius $r$ is embedded in the porous layer. The unit cell is considered with periodic boundary conditions at each right and left edges as described in Section~\ref{sec:method} with fluid radiation on the bottom side and a rigid-backing imposed at the upper boundary. Results with an $r=5$ mm inclusion are presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:small_incl} and are significantly different from those presented in the case of the homogeneous layer, due to the effects of the periodic scatterers. Figure~\ref{fig:small_incl}a depicts the modes originated from $k_1' = 0$ and their symmetric counterparts from $k_1' = 2\pi/d$ which constitutes equivalent solutions, with an opposite phase velocity. The same topology of modes as that of the homogeneous layer are observed, but their frequency evolution is strongly modified. The same classification as that of the homogeneous layer is used for this periodic case. The obtained dispersion relation has some classical features of periodic materials: the $k_1'$ branches in Fig.~\ref{fig:small_incl}b all have a $\pi/d$ periodicity and are folded at a frequency corresponding to the system's Bragg frequency around $k_0 d/ \pi = 1$. No bandgap is opened for this configuration, even though mode I (the only proper mode around the Bragg frequency) presents a maximum attenuation at this location. 

Mode III is still improper as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:homog_layer}. Mode I is folded at a wavenumber $k_1' > \pi/d$ while mode II is folded before reaching the end of the Brillouin zone, seemingly appearing as a wavenumber bandgap around the Bragg frequency might rather be explained by a deformation of the complex plane of the solution due to the losses in the material. This unexpected interaction between the two modes I and II could be explained by the frequency dependence of the JCA model. In further results, the classification of the modes will not be detailed, and mode III will not be represented in the dispersion relations as well as the proper and improper parts of each mode. The case of a similar geometry with fluid radiation at both top and bottom interface is described in Appendix.~\ref{app:transmission}.

\subsection{Rigid inclusion tuned to perfect absorption}

\label{ssec:perfect_absorption}

We consider a unit cell which dimensions are the same as that considered in Section~\ref{ssec:rigid_incl}, i.e., $d=2$ cm and $h = 2$ cm. The acoustic response is computed by adapting the method presented in Section~\ref{sec:method} as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:direct_pb}. The inclusion radius is tuned to obtain perfect absorption at a given frequency. The increase of the radius to $r = 7.3$ mm in this case leads to perfect absorption at normal incidence. This result is presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:perfect_absorption}a, where the absorption coefficient is plotted depending on the frequency and the incident angle. The cases $\theta = 0$°, 45° and 80° are specifically highlighted as line graph in Fig.~\ref{fig:perfect_absorption}e. The absorption peak at $\theta = 0$ is located at $f = 3.2$ kHz. 

The corresponding dispersion relation is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:perfect_absorption}b and Fig.~\ref{fig:perfect_absorption}f, respectively for $k_1'$ and $k_1''$. Using the previous classification introduced in Section~\ref{sec:homog_layer}, we focus on modes I and II. Key differences exist when comparing this dispersion relation to that with the smaller inclusion shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:small_incl}b. While mode II was improper on the whole range of frequencies displayed in the 5 mm radius case, with 7.3 mm radius inclusion this mode is proper from low frequencies to where the branch changes sign around 3.2 kHz, highlighted with the inset of Fig.~\ref{fig:perfect_absorption}f.

Focusing specifically around the frequency $f = 3.2$ kHz a sign change of $k_1''$ for mode II is seen on Fig.~\ref{fig:perfect_absorption}f and three modes are shown on the dispersion relation instead of the two studied so far, this occurs covering a small frequency range from around 3.25 to 3.4 kHz for very low values of $k_1''$. Two branches are in proximity in the wavenumber space, thus said to be hybridized. The branch existing at lower frequencies moves to a different Riemann sheet while another solution emerges as seen on the inset of Fig.~\ref{fig:perfect_absorption}f, that correspond to a trapped mode, which constitutes the cause of existence of the unitary acoustic absorption peak at this frequency. The trapped mode does not show as a single point in the dispersion relation, but rather covers a small frequency range, due to the attenuation of the waves in the structure. Additionally, the mode shape of the hybridized mode in Fig.~\ref{fig:perfect_absorption}d corresponds to the pressure field generated in the structure by the incident field represented in Fig.~\ref{fig:perfect_absorption}c. The mode shape are characteristic of a trapped mode, the pressure field being localized between the upper edge of the inclusion and the rigid backing located $x_2 = 2$ cm. These results are in agreement with prior work focused on a very similar configuration, analysed only through the acoustic response of the structure \cite{groby2011}.

This is not the case here, and since the branches are not folded, those picked up after the end of the Brillouin zone are effectively different solution, corresponding to a different phase velocity sign hence the change of sign of the attenuation.

\subsection{Split-ring resonators}
\label{sec:split_ring_resonator}
\begin{figure*}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/fig_split_ring.pdf}
    \caption{Dispersion relation and absorption curves of the split-ring configurations. Each row represents the $k_1'$ - $f$ diagram with dashed line corresponding to the incident wave angle, $k_1''$ - $f$ diagram, absorption coefficient with the theoretical resonance frequency in dashed lines and mode shapes, respectively. The results in dark blue correspond to the split-ring and those in light red correspond to rigid inclusion with the same radius as the outer radius of the resonator.  a-e) \textit{up1} configuration, f-h) \textit{up2} configuration, k-o) \textit{down1} configuration. The mode shapes shown correspond to the dispersion relation location indicated by the arrows.}
    \label{fig:split_ring}
\end{figure*}

In this section, we propose to study configurations with more complex geometries, in particular with the case of a split-ring resonator (SRR) embedded in the porous unit cell. It was found to provide an additional absorption peak compared to the absorption peak linked to the trapped mode, in configurations where the slit of the resonator is oriented towards the rigid-backing of the structure, involving a coupling. \cite{lagarrigue2013,zhou2019}. Two configurations denoted as \textit{up1} and \textit{up2} are considered with different geometries and the resonator slit facing the rigid backing. The \textit{down1} configuration is shown with the slit facing towards the fluid. The results for the 3 configurations are presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:split_ring} and the parameters for the geometry for each configurations are given in Table~\ref{tab:srr_params} in Appendix~\ref{app:split_ring_params}.   

In each case, the dispersion relation of a rigid inclusion with a radius $r$ equal to that of the outer radius $r_{out}$ of the SRR is also shown, as well as its corresponding absorption coefficient on Figs.~\ref{fig:srr}c, \ref{fig:srr}h and \ref{fig:srr}m for an incident wave with an angle as noted in Table~\ref{tab:srr_params} and excitation line in dashed line in the $k_1'$ diagrams of the configurations, respectively Fig.~\ref{fig:split_ring}a, \ref{fig:split_ring}f, and \ref{fig:split_ring}k. In each case, it can be seen that the absorption coefficient is generally enhanced by the SRR compared to the rigid inclusion and shows two peaks and the second absorption peak frequency corresponds to that of the theoretical SRR resonance frequency, represented by the dashed lines in the absorption plot and computed using the formula given in Appendix~\ref{app:split_ring_params}. 

The \emph{up1} configuration, represented on Figs.~\ref{fig:srr}a to c shows a branch corresponding to mode II (from the classification in Section~\ref{sec:homog_layer}), goes to $k_1'=0$ slightly below 3 kHz and indicated by arrow d), and corresponds to a trapped mode, analogous to that of the perfect absorption case. Compared to the latter, the inclusion size is smaller while the absorption peak is located at a lower frequency. On the other hand, the second peak in the absorption spectrum is caused by a strong shift in the dispersion relation for mode I. At low frequency, its behaviour remains similar to the rigid inclusion configuration. However, this mode hybridizes with the cavity mode around 4.5 kHz, occurring accordingly to the predicted resonance frequency of the SRR. This causes the left-propagating branch (not represented in the plot) and its reciprocal right-propagating branch to cross at $k_1'=0$ as a change of sign in $k_1''$ can be noticed around 4.5 kHz while $k_1'$ goes to 0. In reality, the branch just above marker e) originates from $k_1'<0$ and corresponds to the solution propagating in the other direction. This second absorption peak matches this phenomenon as well as the computed SRR resonance, represented by the dashed lines (formula provided in Appendix~\ref{app:split_ring_params}).

The \emph{up2} geometry reveals, for this case, that only the second peak shows perfect absorption, while the first one remains close to one, but not exactly unitary. This first peak is due to the excitation lying in the vicinity of an avoided crossing of two branches shown by the dispersion relation located by arrow i) on Fig.~\ref{fig:split_ring}. The two branches have a $k_1''$ crossing but the one at $k_1'$ is avoided, hence the very high but not unitary absorption. At this frequency, the two branches cross and lead both to a low attenuation of the wavenumber, thus explaining the large absorption value. The second peak is due to the same phenomenon as the second absorption peak for the \emph{up1} configuration and mode shapes are indeed alike in this case. 

The \emph{down1} configuration shows an absorption spectrum corresponding to an incident wave a $\theta_0 = 10$° incidence angle. In this configuration, the first peak corresponds to the crossing between the excitation line and mode II. Since this is the in-plane mode with a cut-off frequency (mode II) and the excitation line crosses the mode at low frequency, this mode is still strongly attenuated, explaining thus the non-unitary absorption. There, the absorption is similar to the case of the rigid inclusion: at this frequency range, both dispersion relation are alike, explaining the similar results. The second peak is due to the SRR resonance, although the mode shapes are different from the previous two cases as they are not hybridized, it is only the excitation of a mode with low $k_1'$ and low attenuation, remaining far from $k_1'=0$.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
In this work, we have developed a numerical procedure for the computation of the dispersion relation of the leaky guided waves in porous periodic layers with embedded rigid scatterers of arbitrary shape. The dispersion relation allows for an explanations for the modal behaviour of metaporous layers in various configurations. The dispersion relation for a cylindrical inclusion tuned to a perfect absorption peak was shown to highlight a trapped mode. The modification of the inclusion to a split-ring resonator was discussed as well; the dispersion relation allowed to understand the scattering properties of this system in a new light. Three different configurations were studied with varying geometrical parameters: these cases showed two absorption peak for each configuration, explained using the dispersion relation and highlighted different effects such as the hybridisation of a mode with the resonance of the cavity for the \textit{up1} and \textit{up2} configuration, and an avoided crossing close to an exception point in the \textit{up2} configuration. \\

Next steps for this study include the investigation of these metaporous surfaces with heavy fluids both saturating the porous material and loading the structure. The stronger leakage effects could help to understand better these mechanisms and highlight the related behaviour in this work. 

A generalisation of this approach to multilayer systems of arbitrary nature, and more importantly the extension to allow for the modelling of poroelastic-based structures is in progress. The investigation of the contributions of the skeleton motion to the modal behaviour of the structure could provide a better understanding of these structures, while leading to a much more complex modal behaviour as was studied for homogeneous layers \cite{boeckx2005, marechal2025} and periodic structures\cite{wang2020}. 

The dispersion relation evaluation lies on a root-finding procedure aiming to find solutions of the final matrix system from Eq.~\ref{eq:M_det}. While this method is very computationally intensive, it is the only way, as far as we know, to solve the present system. A reframing of the method used for the solution research on might allow for an improvement in numerical performances and reliability of the method. Ideally, the system could be formulated in the form of an eigenvalue problem. Since the final system has a small matrix size, traditional solvers could provide solutions very efficiently. The issue remains the writing of the eigenvalue problem which remains a significant challenge. Other prospects would be in turning to other solvers for such systems including, contour integral methods

K_{\text{eq}} &=\frac{\kappa P_{0}}{\phi}\left(\kappa-(\kappa-1)/\left(1+ \cfrac{\im R_f \phi}{\rho_f \alpha_\infty\text{Pr} \omega} \sqrt{1- \im\text{Pr} \omega \rho_{f} \mu_f\left( \frac{ 2\alpha_{\infty}}{R_f \phi \Lambda^{\prime}}\right)^2 }\right)\right)^{-1}, 
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{widetext}

where $\phi$ is the porosity, $\alpha_{\infty}$ the tortuosity, $\Lambda$ and $\Lambda'$ are respectively the viscous and thermal characteristic lengths, $R_f$ is the flow resistivity, $\mu_f$ is the dynamic viscosity, $\kappa$ is the heat capacity ratio, $\rho_f$ the density of the saturating fluid, and $P_0$ is the ambient pressure.


\subsection{Validation of the method in the case of a layer with rigid boundary conditions}
\label{app:neumann_bc}

\subsubsection{Generalized eigenvalue problem}
With the full problem described in Section.~\ref{sec:method}, writing an eigenvalue problem proves to not be possible. However, in the case where no outgoing wave radiation are considered and replacing the coupling by imposing Neumann boundary conditions at the interface, an eigenvalue problem can be obtained in this specific case. Equation~\ref{eq:periodicity} is rewritten with $\mbf P$ decomposed as $\mbf P = \mbf P_m + \Delta \mbf P_\Delta$ and $\hat {\mbf P} = \mbf{\hat{P}}_m + \Delta^{-1} \mbf{\hat{P}}_\Delta$ such that 
\begin{equation}
    \bigg(\mbf{\hat{P}}_m + \Delta^{-1} \mbf{\hat{P}}_\Delta\bigg) \mbf D \bigg(\mbf P_m +\Delta \mbf P_\Delta\bigg) \mbf p = 0.
\end{equation}
    
Developing the expression yields the form of a polynomial eigenvalue problem of order 2,  
\begin{equation}
    \bigg( \Delta^2 \mbf A_2 + \Delta \mbf A_1 + \mbf A_0 \bigg) p_i = 0,
\end{equation}
with matrices,
\begin{equation}
        \mbf A_2 = \mbf{\hat{P}}_m \mbf D \mbf P_\Delta, \quad \mbf A_1 = \mbf{\hat{P}}_m \mbf D \mbf P_m + \mbf{\hat{P}}_\Delta \mbf D \mbf P_\Delta, \quad \mbf A_0 = \mbf{\hat{P}}_\Delta \mbf D \mbf P_m.
\end{equation}
This can be put into a generalized eigenvalue problem,
\begin{equation}
    \left( \mbf A - \Delta \mbf B \right) \mbf p = 0, \\
\end{equation}
with $\Delta$ the eigenvalue, $\mbf A = \begin{pmatrix}
    -\mbf A_1 & - \mbf A_0 \\ I & 0
\end{pmatrix}, \mbf B  = \begin{pmatrix}
    \mbf A_2 & 0 \\ 0 & I
\end{pmatrix}$, $\mbf p = \begin{pmatrix}
    \Delta \mbf p_l & \Delta \mbf p_i & \mbf p_l & \mbf p_i
\end{pmatrix}^\transp$.

\subsubsection{Comsol model}
Although it has been mentioned in the introduction that Comsol Multiphysics could not be used for modelling the full problem of the present work, it can still be used to validate the method in the case of a layer with rigid boundary conditions. The geometry is thus constituted of the sole unit cell, with Neumann boundary conditions at $\Gamma_t$, $\Gamma_b$. The Helmholtz equation is written as $(\nabla^2 + k^2_{eq})\tilde p(\mbf x) e^{\kappa x_1} = 0$, with the eigenvalue of the problem denoted as $\kappa = -ik_{1,q}$. The coefficient form PDE interface is used for the model, which requires the Helmholtz equation to be written as,
\begin{equation}
    \kappa^2 \tilde p + \nabla \cdot \left(\mbf A_0 \nabla \tilde p + \mbf A_1 \tilde p\right) + \mbf A_1' \nabla \tilde p + k^2_{eq} \tilde p = 0, 
\end{equation}
with $\nabla = \begin{pmatrix}
    \partial_{1} \\ \partial_{2}
\end{pmatrix}$, $\mbf A_0 = \begin{pmatrix}
    1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}$, $\mbf A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} \kappa \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mbf A_1' = \begin{pmatrix} \kappa \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Around the inclusion, rigid boundary conditions are applied, as $-\mbf n \cdot \nabla p - n_2 \cdot \kappa p = 0$ with $\mbf n$ the normal vector to the boundary and $n_2$ its $x_2$ component. At the top and bottom interfaces, the Neumann boundary conditions simply writes as $\partial_2 p(x_1, 0) = 0$ and $\partial_2 p(x_1, H) = 0$. The periodicity is imposed on the right and left boundaries with a continuity condition such that $\tilde p(0, x_2) = \tilde p(d, x_2)$ and since the pressure field $\tilde p$ is defined as a periodic function, this fully imposes the periodicity in the problem. 

\subsubsection{Results}
For the procedure that uses root-finding, the system is constructed following Eq.~(\ref{eq:condenS^tb}) and setting $Q = 0$, since there is no coupling to any semi-infinite medium and thus no need for a Bloch wave series. The system is thus, 
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}% {cc|cc}
    1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
    1 & 0 & T_{11} & T_{21} \\
    0 & 1 & T_{12} & T_{22} 
\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
        S_{1, 0}^t \\ S_{2, 0}^t \\ S_{1, 0}^b \\ S_{2, 0}^b
    \end{pmatrix} = 0,
\end{equation}
with $\mbf T = \left(\mbf M^t\right)^{-1} \mbf M^b$ corresponds to the transfer matrix of the system. The two first rows correspond to the Neumann boundary conditions applied to the system.

For the rigid-rigid case, we therefore present results computed with Comsol as well as with our FEM approach solved using both root-finding and the eigenvalue formulation. All solutions were computed at the same frequencies with a mesh size $\lambda_{min}/10$. While the same size, the Comsol generated mesh might not be identical to that of the mesh generated for the root-finding and eigenproblem methods. In Fig.~\ref{fig:neum}, two modes are obtained: one with lower attenuation is the first propagating mode denoted as 1, and the other one is in-plane with a cut-on frequency denoted as 2. All approaches yield similar results. Moreover, these results are comparable to those obtained in Ref.~\cite{magliacano2020}. 

In Fig.~\ref{fig:neum}c, the absolute error comparing Comsol results and the root-finding method with reference from the GEP is presented. It ensures that the method is able to converge properly to solutions coinciding with the eigenvalues. Note that the general matrices onto which both procedures are done are the same, hence the lower absolute error compared to Comsol which uses significantly different approach.


\subsection{Scattering of the porous periodic cell: addition of an incident wave.}
\label{app:direct_pb}
Adapting the formulation of the problem to that of a forced configuration, the pressure of the bottom pressure field in Eq.~\ref{eq:out_waves} is rewritten to include an incident plane wave field such that, 
\begin{equation}
    p^b(\mbf x) = \sum_{q \in \mathbb Z} \left(\delta_{0q} e^{-\im k_{2, q} x_2} + R_q  e^{+ \im k_{2, q} x_2}\right) \psi_q(x_1),
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{0q}$ is the Kronecker symbol and denotes the amplitude of the incident wave acting only on the 0th Bloch wave. The $a^b$ coefficients are thus denoted as $R_q$, since they become reflection coefficients. The longitudinal wavenumber is thus imposed as $k_1=k_0\mathrm{sin}(\theta)$, with $\theta$ the incidence angle of the plane wave. The same procedure can be applied to derive the matrix system, and the incident wave term is added to Eq.~(\ref{eq:final_system}) as a right-hand side. By solving the system, the amplitude coefficients are obtained and the reflection coefficient is computed as, 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal R = \sum_q \frac{k_{2,q}'}{k_{2, 0}} |R_q|^2.
\end{equation}
The absorption coefficient is thus expressed as $\alpha = 1 - \mathcal R$.

\subsubsection{Effect of the cell stretching in the perfect absorption case}

We want to highlight more precisely the identified behaviour for the perfect absorption configuration in Section~\ref{ssec:perfect_absorption}. \subsection{Details on the split ring configurations}
\label{app:split_ring_params}
The parametrisation of the resonator geometry is presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:srr}, and the parameters for the various configurations covered in the paper are given in Table~\ref{tab:srr_params}. The resonance frequency for each SRR is obtained by adapting the standard equation giving the wavenumber corresponding to the resonance of a Helmholtz resonator \cite{bruneau2006} as $k_r = \sqrt{S/(LV)}$, where $S=\theta_s/(2\pi)$ is the aperture width arc length, $L+\delta \approx r_{out}-r_{in}$ is the neck length and $V = \pi r_{in}^2$ is the cavity surface area. In the present case, the layer wave velocity depends on the frequency. The following expression for the resonance frequency is thus,
\begin{equation}
        f_{res} = \mathrm{min}\left| 1 - \frac{c_{eq}(\omega)W}{\omega} \right|, \quad W = \sqrt{\frac{\left(r_{out} - \cfrac{r_{in}}2\right)\alpha_s}{2\pi (r_{out}- r_{in})r_{in}^2}}.
\end{equation}
