\section{Wave propagation in homogeneous layers}
In the previous section, a formulation for rigid-frame porous media and two formulations for poroelastic media modelling have been presented. Concluding the presentation of these motion equations, some characteristics of the wave propagation in unbounded porous and poroelastic media have been shown. The present section proposes the introduction of a geometry, that is to say boundaries or interface conditions delimiting different domains characterized each by a different type of material. Generally, the configurations studied in this work assume a monochromatic plane wave originated from a fluid half-space, i.e. air, impinging at the surface of a structure and is then scattered (reflected and transmitted) from and through each interface.

If the material in which the wave propagates is bounded by two parallel, free, and flat surfaces, i.e., a plate, then it is refered to as a layer. The waves supported by the medium propagate by reflecting alternatively from one surface to the other; they are said to be guided along the layer. These observations of successive reflections and refractions lead to the concept of waveguides, but their analytical descriptions often leads to writing complex system that can be difficult to solve. To determine the modes of a structure, one must seek to solve the propagation equations and the boundary conditions imposed at its boundaries. In a first section, the scattering of a plane wave by a homogeneous porous layer is investigated along with the various method available. Next, the methods for the computation of the modes of these configurations are presented. In a subsequent section, more complex geometries, those of metaporous structures, are of interest and both methods for the computation of the scattering coefficients and the modes of those configurations will be discussed.

\subsection{Analytical plane wave modelling}
\label{ssec:GM}

Modeling acoustic propagation in homogeneous layers by assuming plane waves is the simplest formalism in acoustics. We assume these plane waves such that they satisfy the motion equations, e.g. the ones introduced in \cref{eq:chap1_helmholtz} or \cref{eq:chap1_biot}. Introducing a simple problem, that of the scattering of an incident plate by a porous layer, we present an example of plane wave modelling for a simple system. The system is 2D with coordinates $\mbf x = (x_1, x_2)$ constituted of a porous layer with thickness $h$ and thus two interfaces of infinite extent along $x_1$, there is a fluid-porous interface at $x_1 = h$ and a porous-fluid interface at $x_1 = 0$. Both media of the air half-spaces and porous layer are modeled as fluid, thus the interfaces conditions should impose continuity of the pressure fields and normal particle displacements. 

Assuming a $ e^{\im \omega t}$ time-dependance, the incident pressure field originated from the upper domain that satisfy the Helmholtz equation takes the form of, 
\begin{equation}
    p_{0+} = a_+ e^{-ik_1 x_1} e^{-ik_{2,0} (x_2 -h)} + a_- e^{-ik_1 x_1} e^{ik_{2,0} (x_2 -h)}.
\end{equation}
The pressure field in the porous layer is expressed as a sum of forward and backward waves,
\begin{equation}
    p_{p} = b_+ e^{-ik_1 x_1} e^{-ik_{2,eq} x_2} + b_- e^{-ik_1 x_1} e^{ik_2 (x_2-h)},
\end{equation}
and that in the lower air domain is,
\begin{equation}
    p_{0-} = c e^{-ik_1x_1}e^{ik_{2,0} x_2},
\end{equation}
with wavenumbers such that they hold for the Snell-Descartes law that states
\begin{equation}
    k_1^2 + k_{2,0}^2 = k_0^2, \,\,\mathrm{ and }\,\, k_1^2 + k_{2,eq}^2 = k_{eq}^2. 
    \label{eq:snell}    
\end{equation}
The wavenumber should be imposed with $k_1 = k_0 \sin{\theta}, k_2 = -k_0 \cos{\theta}$, with $\theta$ the angle of incidence, that governs the direction of propagation of the incident field. The displacement fields are additionally expressed using the momentum equation relating the latter with the pressure fields as $\partial_2 p = -\im \omega \rho_i u_2, i=(0, eq)$. 

Next, the interface conditions describe the relationships between properties at the boundary between two media, including the continuity or vanishing of given fields. If a media is terminated by a surface (e.g. a rigid wall), a constraint should be applied on the fields to represent it, denoted as boundary conditions. Assuming a continuity between the two fluids in the present case with interfaces located at $x_2 = 0$ and $x_2 = h$, we obtain the following conditions,
\begin{equation}
    p^t(h) = p^l(h), \quad u_{2}^t(h) = u_2^l(h) \quad p^l(0) = p^b(0) \quad _{2}^l(0) = u_2^b(0),
\end{equation}
with superscripts $b$ and $t$ denoting respectively the bottom and top interfaces. 

The full analytical description of a system constituted from one or several interfaces can be done by writing a linear system built from the interface conditions, which vector of unknowns are the amplitudes of each of the existing waves in the system, that is $\begin{pmatrix} b_+ & b_- & a_+ & c \end{pmatrix}^\transp$. The forcing right-hand side corresponds to the incident wave terms. The solution of the system yields all unknown amplitudes of the system, in particular the reflection and transmission coefficients. Imposing the interface conditions leads to writing the following matrix system, 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        1 & e^{\im k_{2,eq}h}& 0 & -1 \\
        \cfrac{k_{2,eq}}{\rho_{eq}}& -\cfrac{k_{2,eq}}{\rho_{eq}} e^{\im k_{2,eq}h}&   0& \frac{k_{2,0}}{\rho_0} \\
        e^{\im k_{2,eq}h}& 1 & -1 & 0 \\
        \frac{k_{2,eq}}{\rho_{eq}} e^{\im k_{2,eq}h} &    -\frac{k_{2,eq}}{\rho_{eq}}& -\frac{k_{2,0}}{\rho_{0}}&  0 
    \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} b_+ \\ b_- \\ a_+ \\ c \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
        0 \\ 0 \\ a_- \\ \frac{k_{2,eq}}{\rho_{eq}} a_-
    \end{pmatrix}. 
    \label{eq:gm_pres_layer}
\end{equation}

From this expression, the system can simply be solved to obtain all scattered amplitudes $b_+, b_-, a_+$ and $c$. In the case of a rigidly-backed layer, the interface at $x_2=h$ is replaced by a rigid termination. For (equivalent) fluids, a hard wall or Neumann-type boundary condition is translated by a vanishing normal particle displacement field along the boundary, that is $u_2(\Gamma) = 0$. The term depending on $c$ thus vanishes, since $p_{0-}$ does not exist; this reduces the size of the system. In this case, the reflection coefficient $R$ corresponds to the amplitude $a_+$.

This method is denoted in this work as the \acrfull{gm}, since all of the available data is used in the formulation of the problem as the unknowns are all wave amplitudes. As described in Section\ref{sec:gm_chap_pb_direct} of Chapter \ref{chap:pb_direct}, a general formalism can be used for this method and any stack of any layer can be described with the GM, by putting together a matrix system that contains all interface conditions in the system, in the general form,
\begin{equation}
    \mbf M(\omega) \mbf x = \mbf f,
    \label{eq:sys_gm}
\end{equation}
where $\mbf M$ corresponds to the interface conditions involved in the system, $\mbf x$ contain the wave amplitudes and $\mbf f$ is the forcing term, the incident plane wave amplitudes. 

In the case of a \acrshort{pem}, the interface conditions are, 
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}&\sigma_{12}^{s}=0,\quad\sigma_{22}^{s}+\sigma_{22}^{f}=-p_0,&\sigma_{22}^{f}=-\phi p_0, \quad\phi u^f_{2}+(1-\phi)u^s_{2}=u^f_{2}.\end{aligned}\end{equation}
and for a rigid-backing, 
\begin{equation}
    u^s_1(\Gamma) = 0, \quad u^s_2(\Gamma) = 0, \quad u^f_2(\Gamma) = 0.
\end{equation}\cite{boeckx2005a}

\subsection{Transfer Matrix Method}
The widely used method we know today as the \acrfull{tmm}, was initially developed by the works of Thomson \cite{thomson1950} and Haskell \cite{haskell1953} in order to describe elastic wave propagation in multilayer systems. This method can be applied to a range of wave physics among geophysics, quantum mechanics, electromagnetics and obviously acoustics. \acrshort{tmm} allows for an easy multiphysics coupling with interface matrices; its core lies in the evaluation of the state vector that regroups the variables of interest in the system and evaluate them at each interface, which is possible thanks to the interface conditions. Then the physical field amplitudes stored in the state vector are propagated from one layer to the next. The transfer matrix $\mbf T$ relating the pressure and normal displacement fields from the bottom (located at $x_2 = 0$) to the top interface (located at $x_2 = h$) of a fluid layer is thus, \cite{bruneau2006}

\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        p(0) \\ u_2(0)
    \end{pmatrix} = 
   \underbrace{ \begin{pmatrix}
        \cos(k_{eq}h) & \im Z_{eq} \sin(k_{eq}h) \\
        \im Z_{eq}^{-1} \sin(k_{eq}h) & \cos(k_{eq}h) \\
    \end{pmatrix}}_{\mbf T}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        p(h) \\ u_2(h)
    \end{pmatrix}, 
\end{equation}
where $k_{eq}$ is the bulk wavenumber of the wave propagating in the equivalent fluid and $Z_{eq}$ is the medium impedances. The extension to \acrshort{pem} makes of a state vector that includes the fields, $ \begin{pmatrix} \hat{\sigma}_{12} & u_2^s & u_2^t & \hat{\sigma}_{22} & p & u_1^s \end{pmatrix}^\transp$. More details are given in Chapter 11 of Allard and Atalla textbook \cite{allard2009}.

For stacks of layers, an amount of $N$ layers of the same nature are easy to manipulate since $\mbf T_{\mathrm{global}} = \prod_{n=0}^N \mbf T_n$ since they have the same size. multiphysics with interface matrix that relate the relation between the various fields at an interface (non square matrices). This method was deep dived in several textbooks among which we propose Refs.~\cite{allard2009, jimenez2021} for additional details. The TMM also allows for the computation of complex system by an assembly of elements including admittance matrices in order to consider \acrfull{hr} embedded in an equivalent fluid layer \cite{doutres2015}.

While very versatile, it remains unstable in limit cases at high frequencies or for large thickness of the layers. While the formulation is mathematically exact, the evaluation of the system involves involves finite-precision calculation which leads the very sensitive exponential terms to diverge in the previously specified regimes. From the evaluation of the transfer matrix of the global system, the scattering matrix can be obtained and thus the reflection and transmission coefficients by simply manipulating the expressions for the fields contained in the state vector with the scattering coefficients. 

Considering a rigidly-backed layer, and thus $u_2(h) = 0$, we obtain a reflection coefficient,
\begin{equation}
    R_b =\frac{T_{11}-Z_{eq}T_{21}}{T_{11}+Z_{eq}T_{21}}.
\end{equation}

In the case of the symmetric layer, the reflection and transmission coefficients are determined from the scattering matrix as well, 
\begin{equation}
    T_s = \frac{e^{-ikL}}{T_{11}+T_{12}/Z_{eq}+Z_{eq}T_{21}+T_{22}}, \quad
    R_s = \frac{T_{12}/Z_{eq}-Z_{eq}T_{21}}{T_{11}+T_{12}/Z_{eq}+Z_{eq}T_{21}+T_{22}}.
\end{equation}
which is only valid for reciprocal, time-invariant and linear assumptions. The absorption coefficient for the same two configurations are calculated as $\alpha_b = 1 - |R_b^2|$ and $\alpha_s = 1 - |R_s^2| - |T_s^2|$ and are shown in Figs~\ref{fig:poroelastic_scattering_ex}c and d with markers. It can be seen that the same results (up to the respective numerical precision of each method) are obtained. 

\section{Guided waves for dispersion problems}
In this section, we take an interest in similar configurations as previously, removing the excitation or forcing term in the equations. Solving the appropriate set of equations for a given set of parameters leads to the modes of the free system, that is the solution of the characteristic equation leading a specific behaviour of the wave propagation in the structure for the said parameters. The form of the modes of wave propagation in layer is discussed and various methods for their calculation of are presented.

The existence of an interface in the geometry leads to the propagation of not only the bulk waves but also of so-called surface waves \cite{auld1973} where the interface itself acts as the support for propagation. Different types of waves propagate along the interface. An example can be water waves that follow the interface between air and water; most of the energy is localized at the interface. A similar behaviour exists for seismic waves where the interface of a solid substrate supports the propagation of Rayleigh (longitudinal motion) and Love waves (transverse motion) which both show an energy strongly decaying with depth. Scholte waves are waves that propagate along the interface between a fluid and a solid medium. In fluid layers, the propagating waves are similar to studying a shear horizontal (SH) wave in an elastic layer. One mode propagates at zero frequency, while countless other modes propagate with a cut-off frequency that corresponds to the successive quarter-wavelength resonances, essentially depending on the layer thickness and the nature of the boundary/interface conditions.  

From the previously obtained linear systems in Section~\ref{ssec:GM} for the configurations, and removing the incident wave from the system, i.e. the forcing (right-hand side) term, an homogeneous linear system is obtained, in the general form $\mathcal L(\mbf k, \omega) \mbf s = 0$, where the operator $\mathcal L$ governs wave propagation in the structure, $\mbf k = (k_1, k_2)^\transp$ is the wavenumber vector in the 2D geometry and $\mbf s$ a vector containing the amplitude of all the partial waves. In order to obtain nontrivial solutions for $\mbf s$, the system should satisfy a vanishing determinant for the linear system, $\mathrm{det}(\mbf L (\mbf k, \omega)) = 0$. The parameters satisfying this so-called \emph{dispersion equation} can be sought, allowing to identify the modes of the structure. These equations can be solved for various parameter spaces depending on the perpective adopted on the problem \cite{ducasse2022, deschamps2025}: solving for frequencies while imposing the wavenumber or the other way around. The first one is useful when interested in the time-dependant effects the structure has on the propagation. In this work, we are interested in the losses generated by the structure and the material and as such frequency is fixed in order to solve complex wavenumbers. The solutions are thus able to indicate on rather the spatially induced dispersion occuring in the system.

\subsection{A bestiary of the wave solutions in dissipative and open waveguides}
In this section, an overview of the possible sets of solutions for the guided waves in waveguide is presented. These sets of solutions are determined by the direction of the propagation, the attenuation and the nature of the leaky wave if it exists. This \emph{bestiary} is split into 3 categories. For all cases, the focus is on the values taken by the real (denoted by $'$) and imaginary parts (denoted by $''$) of each wavenumber component. Considering a layer and half-space system, there exists three wavenumber components from Eq.~(\ref{eq:snell}). The form of solutions are assumed as 
\begin{equation}
   p_l \propto e^{-ik_1'x_1}e^{+k_1''x_1} e^{\pm ik_{2,eq}'} e^{\mp k_{2,eq}''x_2}, \quad p_0 \propto e^{-ik_1'x_1}e^{+k_1''x_1} e^{+ik_{2,eq}'} e^{k_{2,eq}''x_2}.
\end{equation}
The longitudinal wavenumber is the same in both layer and half-space domain as a consequence of the Snell-Descartes law and the continuity equations: there exists in total three dependant wavenumber components in this system, $k_1$, $k_{2,0}$ and $k_{2,eq}$ among which determining two allows to compute all,
\begin{equation}
    k_{2,0} = \pm \sqrt{k_0^2 - k_1^2}, \quad k_{2,eq} = \pm \sqrt{k_{eq}^2 - k_1^2}. 
\end{equation}
Because of the reciprocal nature of the presented configurations, and the considerations of unbounded layers in the $x_1$ direction, a plane of solutions corresponding to $k_1' \geq 0$ can be selected, as the right-going solution is perflectly equivalent solutions to the left-going solutions.  

In \textbf{conservative and closed systems}, the study is restricted to only the two wavenumber components in the layer, since no half-space is accounted. The total acoustic energy is thus confined to the structure enclosed by its boundaries.  A type of wave solution exists as \textit{perfectly guided}, this propagating wave has $k_1' > 0$ with no attenuation $k_1'' = 0$. Solutions include \textit{purely evanescent} wave as well $k_1'' > 0$ which often exists and continuates a propagating mode below its cut-off frequency.

\textbf{Open systems} supports the existence of the previous types of modes with additional phenomena. Since part of the energy is susceptible to radiate out from the plate, the energy is no longer confined to the layer. Some of the previously purely guided modes can thus become attenuated, $k_1'' > 0$. Waves are still \textit{perfectly guided} with conditions $k_{20}' = 0$ with evanescent waves in the fluid $k_{20}'' < 0$, meaning that waves are confined to the proximity of the layer interface. The additional condition $k_1' = 0$ correspond to \textit{trapped modes}, which are a set of discrete eigenvalues of the system. They can exist in waveguides where the system is confined in one direction at least, the energy can thus be localized around some obstacle or defect by the evanescent waves \cite{pagneux2013, roitberg1998}. In open systems, they correspond to  waves that are evanescent at infinity. However, in open waveguides, the acoustic energy is usually able to radiate outside of the waveguide, leading to two type of waves as \textit{leaky waves} in the case $k_{20}' > 0$ and \textit{incoming waves} (also called backward leaky) with $k_{20}' < 0$. These solutions are respectively exponentially growing as it radiates $k_{20}'' > 0$ or attenuated $k_{20}'' < 0$. These at first counterintuitive results ensue from the requirements that the wave should be propagating $k_1' > 0$ and attenuated $k_1'' < 0$ along the layer, and also from the relation, $k_1'k_1'' = -k_{20}' k_{20}''$ obtained by taking the imaginary part of the dispersion equation \cite{treyssede2014}. We refer also to the thesis of Kiefer \cite{kiefer2022}, especially Section 3.2 dedicated to the form of solutions for quasi-guided waves in the case of an elastic layer coupled to a semi-infinite fluid half-space. 

The addition of \textbf{dissipation in open systems}leads to the mixing of several loss mechanisms: the leakage of waves and the dissipations within the guide (which can be of whatever nature). In this case, the separation between incoming and leaky waves is not possible anymore. This particular aspect has been very scarcely tackled in the literature: an unpublished paper is interested in an elastic waveguide \cite{kiefer2020} while in optics, a paper discusses the waves guided in nanospheres with a complex permittivity \cite{campione2011}. There, the modes are sorted as \textit{proper} and \textit{improper} solutions. This complex case is covered in a dedicated section \ref{sec:classification} in chapter \ref{sec:prb}.

\subsection{Computing the modes of porous layers using semi-analytical tools}
\label{ssec:semi-analyt-layers}
The literature on the dispersion relation of porous/poroelastic-based structures is scarce; Groby \etal \cite{groby2008} have shown the complex dispersion relation in a homogeneous equivalent fluid layer coupled to air while Boeckx \etal\cite{boeckx2005} have computed the modes propagating in a poroelastic waveguide coupled at each top and bottom interface with an outgoing plane wave propagating in an air medium. This has been done using an analytical description of the system by expressing the interface conditions in a linear system (which we call \acrshort{gm} in this work). Both of these work have used a root-finding procedure in order to solve the modes of the structure.

Once the linear system is obtained, we are faced with either or two of these possibilities. Solving wavenumbers can be done through the writing and solving of a (possibly generalized) eigenvalue problem or by root-finding of solutions in the case where writing the first one appears impossible. 

Using Eq.~(\ref{eq:sys_gm}), root finding such that $\mathrm{det}\left(\mbf M(k_1, \omega)\right) = 0$; 
the determinant of the matrix from Eq.~(\ref{eq:gm_pres_layer}) can be expressed as 
\begin{equation}
    2 \frac{k_{2,eq} k_{2,0}}{\rho_{eq} \rho_{0}} \text{cos}(k_{2,eq} h) + \im \left(\frac{k_{2,eq}^2}{\rho_{eq}^2} + \frac{k_{2,0}^2}{\rho_{0}^2}\right) \text{sin}(k_{2,eq} h) = 0.
\end{equation}

From this expression, one can use a root-finding procedure to solve for either the term $k_{2,eq}$ or $k_1$ to obtain the dispersion relation by varying the frequency. Results are shown in \cref{fig:porous_disp}

\subsection{Numerics for modes computation}
\label{ssec:numerics-layers}
Several numerical methods have been investigated in the last decades with the goal of computing complex modes in homogeneous layers. Guided waves are of interest in a wide range of geometries and we focus here on the case of simple geometries, that are unbounded single (or stack of) layers with two infinite interfaces as tackled in the previous sections. 

Even if a semi-analytical description of the system is possible, it can be advantageous to be able to write the problem differently, as root-finding routines often lead to challenges as discussed in earlier sections. With that in mind, several numerical methods have been introduced with the same framework: a 1D discretization of the layer along the thickness and assuming harmonic motion along the longitudinal direction. These methods are entitled \acrshort{scm} and \acrshort{safe}. Spectral methods have been around for quite some time and have been used for many numerical applications. We focus for this work on \acrfull{scm} \cite{trefethen2000}, which has been especially used for the computation of guided wave modes in elastic layers\cite{adamou2004, kiefer2019, georgiades2022}. We refer to the introduction of Chapter~\ref{chap:jap} for an extensive bibliography on \acrshort{scm} applications.

This method is useful for the discretization of 1D geometries as is the object of the present section. 

In the case of simple 2D geometries, a 1D discretization along the transverse direction associated to a harmonic motion in the longitudinal direction. Even if the use of finite elements seems farfetched, this method could be relevant in contexts where boundary effects or guide with varying cross-sections are studied. However, a variation of finite elements based on a 1D discretization of the waveguide is entitled \acrfull{safe} and is akin to \acrshort{scm} which we have described in the previous paragraph. The 1D discretization is done along the thickness of the layer by regular nodes, and the fields are interpolated using finite elements shape functions. Examples of \acrfull{safe} use cases are very similar to \acrshort{scm} and include \comment{lister des publis ici}. Examples are also given in Chapter \ref{chap:jap}. A detailed description of the calculation of Lamb modes using a simple SAFE implementation using high-order Lobatto polynomials is proposed in Appendix.~\ref{app:safe_vs_scm}. There, its performance is also compared to that of SCM.

There is a discussion to be made on the relevance of the imposition of a local coupling contrary to a full modelling of the surrounding media with some termination condition satisfying the Sommerfeld condition, PML or analogous absorbing layer. While leading to no assumptions on the radiating field nature contrary to imposing a local coupling, the modeling of the half-space by using some artificial absorbing layer at the end leads to the apparition of modes of the cavities \cite{gallezot2017}. 

We argue that the advantages of the imposition of a local coupling is two-fold: less degrees of freedom are necessary since the sole interface is modeled, not the whole half-space. For dispersion problems, this is particularly interesting since we directly obtain leaky mode solutions, without needing to filter out all radiation modes linked to the additional cavity and the resonances of the PML.
